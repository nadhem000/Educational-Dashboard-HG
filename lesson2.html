WWI: Causes

Introduction

World War I (1914‑1918) was a global conflict that reshaped the political map of the world. To understand its outbreak, historians examine long‑term tensions – militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism – and a single spark that ignited the powder keg. This lesson explores those intertwined causes.

I) Causes of World War I

1. Intensified Colonial Rivalry

A. Rivalry between European colonial powers intensified in the last quarter of the 19th century due to several reasons:

• The maturation of the Industrial Revolution and Europe's growing need for external markets to export surplus industrial production.

• The European economy suffered the "Great Depression" between 1873 and 1886, reducing profits on investment and forcing a search for new outlets for capital.

• The emergence of new European powers such as Germany and Italy, which demanded a re‑division of the world and their right to colonies.

B. Anglo‑German rivalry intensified when Germany's Emperor Wilhelm II adopted a new economic policy called "Weltpolitik" (world policy), which believed Germany's future lay on the water. He strengthened the German navy and expanded economic influence into the Ottoman Empire and Asia, alarming Britain.

C. The colonial rivalry led to international crises that almost turned into wars:

• The Fashoda Incident (1898) between France and Britain, resolved by the Entente Cordiale.

• The Moroccan Crises between Germany and France: Tangier (1905) and Agadir (1911), settled by compromise – France ceded part of Congo to Germany in exchange for a protectorate over Morocco (1912).

2. The Alliance System and the Arms Race

A. Alliances:

• Triple Alliance: Germany allied with Austria‑Hungary (1879) to isolate France and prevent it from recovering Alsace‑Lorraine. Italy joined in 1882, angered by the French protectorate over Tunisia (1881). Italy later signed a secret neutrality pact with France (1902) and left the alliance in 1915, joining the Entente after the secret Treaty of London promised territorial gains. The alliance later included the Ottoman Empire (1914) and Bulgaria (1915).

• Triple Entente: To break its isolation, France formed agreements with Britain (1904) and Russia. The Entente aimed to encircle Germany and end French isolation. It later expanded to include Japan (1914), Romania, Portugal, Italy (1915), and the United States (1917).

B. Arms race:

• Military service was extended from two to three years; army sizes soared. By 1913, Germany had 820,000 soldiers and Russia 1.8 million. Naval rivalry between Britain and Germany escalated. Defence budgets and military spending multiplied.

3. Nationalism in Central Europe and the Balkans

Tensions in the region stemmed from:

• The presence of multiple ethnic groups (Slavs, Czechs, Hungarians) seeking independent states, especially the South Slavs supported by Serbia.

• Rivalry between Russia (which wanted access to warm waters via the Bosphorus) and Austria‑Hungary (which aimed to reach the Adriatic by occupying Bosnia‑Herzegovina in 1908).

The resulting tension led to the Balkan Wars (1912‑1913) and the growth of Slavic nationalism.

4. The Sarajevo Incident and the Outbreak of the Great War

On 28 June 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro‑Hungarian throne, was assassinated by a Bosnian student belonging to a Slavic nationalist movement.

Austria blamed Serbia and demanded to send judges to participate in the investigation; Serbia refused, considering it a violation of its sovereignty.

Encouraged by Germany, Austria‑Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July 1914. Through the alliance system, the conflict escalated into a world war. Other states joined the alliances: Japan (1914), Italy (1915), Romania, Portugal, and the USA (1917) sided with the Entente; the Ottoman Empire (1914) and Bulgaria (1915) joined the Central Powers.

Conclusion

World War I was not caused by a single event but by a combination of deep‑seated factors: the arms race, the alliance system, colonial rivalries, and fervent nationalism. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand provided the immediate trigger, setting off a chain reaction through the alliances that plunged Europe into war.

Definitions

Militarism
The belief that a country should maintain a strong military and be prepared to use it aggressively.
Alliance System
A formal agreement between nations to cooperate and support each other, especially in war.
Imperialism
A policy of extending a country's power through diplomacy or military force, often by acquiring colonies.
Nationalism
A strong sense of pride and devotion to one's nation, sometimes leading to the desire for independence or dominance.
